All-cause deaths
Unknown
Battle casualties (killed/wounded)

What the record supports

Smallpox was a serious constraint during the Continental Army’s Canada campaign. Contemporary correspondence treated it as a major military danger.

Limits of interpretation

A participant’s emphatic assessment is evidence of perception and concern, not a measured fraction of defeat attributable to disease.

Morristown: the inoculation decision 1777-02-06

1777-02-06 · Inoculation policy

Unknown
All-cause deaths
Unknown
Battle casualties (killed/wounded)
Not applicable

What the record supports

Washington instructed William Shippen to arrange smallpox inoculation for troops in Philadelphia and those coming through.

Limits of interpretation

This was variolation using smallpox material, before Jennerian vaccination. An order establishes policy; implementation and outcomes need additional records.

Evidence and sources

  • George Washington to William Shippen, Jr., 6 February 1777Reviewed source text · Source date: 1777-02-06

All-cause deaths
Unknown

What the record supports

Washington told Grier to send recruits who had not had smallpox to Philadelphia for inoculation, while those with prior infection could join the army.

Limits of interpretation

The letter specifies a workflow. It does not quantify how many recruits completed it or its causal effect on mortality.

Evidence and sources

  • George Washington to David Grier, 12 March 1777: smallpox inoculation letterReviewed source text · Source date: 1777-03-12

What the record supports

The National Park Service estimates nearly 2,000 disease deaths during the encampment. Its account places many deaths in the warmer spring months.

Limits of interpretation

The figure is an approximate aggregate, not a diagnosis-specific count or a constant-risk population rate. “Winter encampment” includes spring; this is not a count of freezing deaths.
